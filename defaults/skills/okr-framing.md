OKRs (Objectives and Key Results; Doerr, *Measure What Matters*, 2018, from the Intel/Google lineage) frame goals as a qualitative aspiration paired with quantitative evidence of progress. The structure separates *where we want to go* from *how we will know we got there*.

## Objective

A single, memorable, qualitative statement of the outcome to achieve in the period. It should be ambitious and directional — not a task, not a metric. "Make onboarding effortless" is an objective; "ship the new signup form" is a task.

## Key Results

Each objective carries two to five Key Results, and every KR is a metric with three parts:

- **Baseline** — the current measured value. Without it, the target is unanchored.
- **Target** — the value that signals success, set to be hard but achievable.
- **Timeframe** — the window in which the target is measured.

A KR phrased as an activity ("run three experiments") is a task masquerading as a result; rewrite it as the outcome the activity is meant to produce.

## Discipline

KRs measure outcomes, not output. If every KR can be satisfied by completing a checklist regardless of impact, the framing has failed. Review progress against the baseline through the period; OKRs are a steering instrument, not a year-end report.

## Boundaries

OKRs set direction and success signals; they do not prioritise or sequence the work beneath them. Feed the objective into prioritisation (MoSCoW/RICE) and the KRs into acceptance criteria where individual deliverables need a checkable bar.
